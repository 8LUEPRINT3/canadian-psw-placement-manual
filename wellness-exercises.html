Why this matters for PSWs

Personal Support Work is physically demanding: lifting and transferring clients, repositioning in bed, bending, and standing or walking for 8–12 hour shifts. That makes two things non-negotiable:

  • Injury prevention — lower-back strain is the #1 work-related injury among care workers. A strong core + correct body mechanics is your best protection.
  • Alertness — early starts, rotating shifts, and nights disrupt energy and sleep. Short, deliberate movement resets your focus better than another coffee.

Every routine below is body-weight only — no equipment, no gym, doable in a break room or at home.

🚧 Bending at the waist

Spine as a lever — load amplifies at L4–L5

  • Load concentrated on the lumbar discs
  • Hamstrings + back do all the work
  • Higher risk of disc strain

✅ Squatting, neutral spine

Spine as a crane — load spreads to glutes & quads

  • Load distributed across the big leg muscles
  • Core braced, spine stays neutral
  • Your strongest, safest lifting position

Same weight, two very different loads on your back. Always choose the crane.

Your working muscles — a quick map

  • Shoulders — lifting, rolling, and steadying during transfers
  • Core — the brace that protects your spine on every lift
  • Quads & glutes — the squat-and-lift engine (your "crane")
  • Calves — stability on your feet all shift
  • Back (not shown) — trained by hip hinge + bird-dog, the anti-rounding muscles

PRE-SHIFT Warm-up — 5 minutes

Before you touch a client. Gets blood flowing and wakes up your hips, back, and shoulders.

  • March in place — 60 seconds
  • Shoulder rolls + arm circles — 10 each direction
  • Hip circles — 10 each way
  • Standing spine stretch — reach up, then fold forward slowly, 5×
  • Ankle rolls + calf raises — 10–15 each
  • Gentle body-weight squats — 5–10, slow, knees over toes

MID-SHIFT Alertness resets — 2–3 minutes

Do one of these on a break, or between tasks when you feel the energy dip. No equipment, no sweat.

  • Brisk walk or stairs — 2 minutes, get daylight if you can
  • 10 body-weight squats or a 30–60s wall sit — fires up the legs and spine
  • Standing hip + back stretch — counterpose the lifting you've been doing
  • Box breathing — inhale 4s, hold 4s, exhale 4s, hold 4s; 4–6 rounds
  • Neck + shoulder release — slow rolls, 30s
  • Splash cold water on your face / wrists — instant reset

POST-SHIFT Recovery — 5–10 minutes

Unwind the muscles you used all shift, before you sit down for the evening.

  • Child's pose — 30–60s, breathe into the lower back
  • Hamstring stretch — seated or standing, 30s per side
  • Hip-flexor stretch (lunge stretch) — 30s per side; counteracts all that standing
  • Foam rolling — back, glutes, quads if you have a roller
  • Slow breathing / wind-down — 5 slow breaths, let the nervous system downshift

OFF-DAY Strength routine — 15–20 minutes, 2–3×/week

This is the routine that actually prevents back injuries. Rest 30–60s between sets. Focus on form over speed.

  • Body-weight squats — 3 × 10–15
  • Lunges — 3 × 8–10 per side
  • Glute bridges — 3 × 12
  • Bird-dog — 3 × 10 per side (core + balance)
  • Plank — 3 × 20–45s
  • Wall sit — 3 × 30–60s
  • Calf raises — 3 × 15
  • Hip hinge (unweighted) — 3 × 10; teaches the "squat, don't bend" pattern you use to lift

Back care — the part that ties it all together

Strength is only half of it. How you move on the floor is what keeps your back safe:

Keep the load close
Close to your center of gravity, not at arm's length
Pivot with your feet
Move your whole body — never twist the spine under load
Brace your core
Tighten as if about to be poked, before you lift

Tap each one as you master it on the floor:

  • ✓Bend your knees, not your waist — squat down to the client's level instead of hinging over.
  • ✓Keep the load close — hug the client/object to your body before you move.
  • ✓Brace your core — a quick "tighten as if about to be poked" before any lift.
  • ✓Pivot with your feet — never twist your spine while holding weight.
  • ✓Use your tools — gait belts, transfer aids, and lifts exist for a reason. Never lift alone if the load is heavy.
  • ✓Alternate tasks and take micro-breaks — variety and short pauses prevent repetitive strain.
